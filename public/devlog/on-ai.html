Philosophical prelude

The title's allusions are from Solzhenitsyn's "One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich" and Camus' "The Myth of Sisyphus" respectively (and kinda uncreatively).

It's flippant to make such an allusion given the amount of human suffering involved, but I was reminded of Ivan Denisovich while thinking about this stuff; specifically the immense pride the character takes in laying the bricks straight and mixing the mortar properly despite the fundamental futility of the labor as he's doing it in a gulag. (Craftsmanship is a flame of human agency that shields against spiritual despair.)

There is a universality here (as all things must have). Anything we do (game dev included) is a reminder of the inherent conflict between a deep desire for intrinsic meaning and an indifferent universe. The absurdity of that, that there is no ultimately correct or prescribed path, forces us to either live authetically or in bad faith. Living truly by your own self-created values or conforming to societal expectations or the dogmas you inherited.

The tiger sniffed at him from above. Trembling, the man looked down to where, far below, another tiger was waiting to eat him. Only the vine sustained him. Grasping the vine with one hand, he plucked the strawberry with the other. How sweet it tasted!

Regarding Agentic workflows or the insistence that we're at a higher abstraction layer

It's important not to make a categorical error about AI (which seems to be the dominant mistake amongst techy AI boosters). It's not an abstraction layer. An abstraction layer (i.e. machine code to assembly or assembly to high level language) is a deterministic translation. Sampling tokens based on hyperspace probability distributions is a stochastic process (not deterministic).

Agents are thus, at a fundamental level, chains of discrete probabilistic decisions. Imagine a task broken down into 20 discrete steps, Even if an individual agentic step has an extraordinarily high success rate of 95% the probability that the entire 20-step workflow executes without a single error is calculated as \(0.95^{20}\). That overall success rate plummets to ~36%. So, more than 6 out of 10 runs will fail purely due to the compounding math of sequential probabilities.

Unlike a deterministic abstraction layer where a bad instruction halts execution immediately (e.g., a syntax error that stops a compiler), a probabilistic agent handles errors improvisationally which exponentially compounds and causes "semantic drift" This can lead to exponential spiraling if it hits a hard barrier (testing barrier) and break invariant code elsewhere in codebase to self-correct, causing development thrashing and a proper mess.

I think we're touching an irreducible fault line here. Code generation (or anything generation) is stochastic and is fundamentally unreliable, thus guardrails (harnesses) are made that deterministically stear the generation, but if those gaurdrails themselves are generated by the same stochastic processes they are meant to police, they share the exact same blind spots, latent biases, and hallucination vectors as the code they are checking (thus there has to be a prime mover / axiom i.e. a human that at least kind of understands what the hell's going on, something that is able to jump out of a formal system)

More Mark Jivko on the topic This kind of touches some deep philosophical ground (Godel's incompletes theorem & Baudrillardian hyperreality)

A more valid approach I've seen brough up recently from AI boosters is an anology to evolution, that evolution is a random (stochastic) process and that if it works it works (just let the generative process explore a design or utility space until it does what I want it to do) I can kind of buy into this at first, but it also seems a lot weaker when you consider the driver of evolution in the real world (biological or otherwise) is the immutable laws of physics. I don't think there is a corresponding evolutionary pressure in software. A piece of software can be completely broken, logically incoherent, and full of security backdoors, yet still compile, pass a poorly written test suite, and run happily for months until a specific sequence of user inputs triggers a catastrophe

Without explanatory understanding, the system accumulates accidental complexity at an exponential rate and is fragile to future incongruous interaction (something always comes up and without understanding the whole it can never be sucessfully subsumed). There is a differnce between knowing something and understanding it You cannot outsource understanding; there is no such thing as passive learning.

I'm not anti AI (I'm actually super pro AI if done in a more sustainable (with respect to the environment) and moral way, revolting against the consolidating of power / technofeudalism / bro-ligarchy and rent seeking behavior) I just think we're currently creating a large categorical mistake pushed by shitty, stupid (fundamentally uninteresting to me, i.e. money hungry) tech bros / managerial class who are capitalizing an uncertain technological trajectory as though it were a nearly certain economic outcome.

Do not diminish the value of your own humanity

In the same way that LLMs are a high dimensional space searching tool, a non-trivial human product carries with it a high dimensional imprint of all the thousands of decisions and interactions that allowed it to take on the shape that it is.

This is true also of skill development and gaining udnerstanding, they can neither be entirely reductionistically defined nor offloaded to an abstraction layer. These are mutually exclusive with automation. You get better by doing, there's simply no substitute for it.

So, should you be trying to make video games in this new world?

Steam is more accessible than it's every been, and building a game for it is riskier than it's ever been. Better tools, easier publishing => Less barrier to entry => naturally more games being made and released. This could fall within Jevon's paradox, "making a resource more efficient to use leads to an overall increase in total consumption rather than a decrease", or it could be more along the lines of the video game crash of 1983 (extreme market saturation with predominantly poor quality)

And they introduced protection. The 10NES lockout chip and the Official Nintendo Seal of Quality. You could not just release a cheap game anymore. They controlled quality and quantity.
